A hands-on explorer for one question: when a video shows something physically impossible — an object passing through a wall, or vanishing behind one and never coming back — does the AI notice? We read out the model's own “surprise”: how wrong its internal prediction of the next moment turns out to be. The most useful part of this project isn't a single score — it's the Explorer, where you can watch that surprise light up on the actual frames.

How to read the Explorer

Open the Examples tab and pick a scene. You get a matched pair of clips — identical except for one physical event — playing in lockstep, with the model's reaction drawn underneath. Here's what each element means:

  • Possible clip (left) — the physically normal version of the scene.
  • Impossible clip (right) — the same scene with one impossible event spliced in (a teleport, a disappearance, an object passing through a solid block).
  • The surprise curves — under the videos, two lines (blue = possible, red = impossible) trace the model's surprise frame by frame. A playhead rides both curves as the clips play, so you can see where in time surprise rises — or fails to.
  • Surprise heatmap (toggle it on) — paints each frame with the model's surprise per patch, bright where the model finds that part of the image most unexpected. This is the centerpiece: it shows what the model is reacting to, not just how much.
  • “Patches that differ” outlines — mark the small region where the impossible event actually happens, so you can compare it against where the heatmap lights up.
  • Pixel-diff band (orange) — a faint band showing where the two clips simply differ in raw pixels. It's an honesty check: that's what's visually different, which is not necessarily where the physics breaks.

What you'll notice

  • The surprise heatmap often does land on the right spot — the place where the impossible thing happens — especially in simple, uncluttered scenes. Watching that happen is the most compelling part of the tool.
  • It's less reliable on harder scenes: more objects, more motion, more occlusion. There the bright patches tend to drift toward whatever is moving rather than whatever is impossible.
  • That tension previews the headline result below — a lot of what looks like “surprise at impossibility” turns out to be surprise at motion and appearance.

The headline result, stated plainly

If you boil each clip down to a single surprise number and ask “was the impossible clip more surprising than its possible twin?”, the answer is basically a coin flip. Across 180 matched pairs the accuracy sits near chance (0.50), and which side of chance you land on depends only on how you average the numbers. Control experiments (NULL_CONTROLS.md) show the surprise tracks how much is moving in the frame (r = 0.855 with raw motion energy) far more than whether physics was violated. So as a yes/no “did it break physics?” detector, this checkpoint does not work — and we report that honestly. The Explorer is what makes the finding legible: you can see the surprise following motion instead of impossibility.

Addendum — a correction worth stating

Our first instinct was to call this a fact about the method. It isn't. Two checks changed the story. First, when we ran our exact scoring on the original authors' published surprise numbers, we reproduced their ~92% accuracy to the decimal — so our analysis pipeline is sound. Second, the same network architecture loaded with a different model's weights (V-JEPA 1, trained on how-to videos) scores ~92% on this very benchmark under our pipeline. So the near-chance result is a fact about this particular checkpoint (V-JEPA 2 ViT-L) — not about the approach, the architecture, or world-models in general. The “surprise = motion detector” story is true of the model we probed, and the Explorer shows you exactly why. Full detail in CROSS_CHECK.md and RECONCILIATION.md.
